Executive summary

Group work, Laamanen CAS M&A module (27 Nov 2026): design an acquisition program for Legend Holdings (HKEX: 3396), with AI technology as the investment focus. This page covers all five design elements: strategic vision and acquisition logic, the program opportunities, the target landscape for Program B in Europe, a five-year acquisition sequence, and governance with stop rules.

1 · Strategic vision and acquisition logic

Who Legend Holdings is today

  • Model: a two-wheel holding company, "industrial operations" plus "industrial incubation and investment". Operating subsidiaries: Lenovo Group (smart devices and infrastructure), Levima Group (new materials), Banque Internationale à Luxembourg (BIL, banking) and Joyvio Group (agriculture and food).
  • Scale: 2025 revenue RMB 605.9bn (+18%), net profit RMB 9.8bn (+28%), R&D above RMB 17bn, nearly 150 Chinese tech companies invested in during the year. H1 2026 revenue RMB 362.9bn (+29%), attributable net profit RMB 2.23bn (+219%), cash RMB 79.5bn.
  • Strategy: repositioned as a science-and-technology group. In H1 2026 it added more than 80 investments in AI, optical interconnect, quantum, embodied intelligence, new materials, smart healthcare, commercial aerospace and the low-altitude economy.
  • AI footprint: more than 300 AI companies invested across the Legend family, including Zhipu, Moore Threads, MetaX, Pony.ai and Cambricon. 70+ portfolio companies work commercially with Lenovo.
  • Lenovo is the engine: Legend raised its stake from 31.41% to 32.34% in late 2025. In H1 2026 Lenovo's infrastructure revenue grew 62% with a US$54bn AI server backlog, and AI-related revenue rose 64% to 36% of the total.
  • Portfolio pruning: Legend is reported (Financial Times, Sep 2026) to be working with Goldman Sachs on selling its 90% BIL stake at a valuation of about €2.5bn or more.

2 · Acquisition program opportunities: the three programs chosen

A. Technology access: a minority-to-majority ladder

  • Logic: use the information advantage of 300+ AI stakes. Over five years, raise Legend's holding to control (51%+) in 2–4 portfolio companies that have shown product-market fit, focusing on embodied intelligence and AI hardware (edge AI chips, optical interconnect). Buy around the late-stage or pre-IPO round, with Legend's funds as co-sellers.
  • Why it fits: lowest search cost and best due diligence, because Legend already sits on these boards. Its record (15 portfolio listings in 2025, 12 in H1 2026) gives a clear exit-or-control decision per holding.
  • Risks and mitigation:
    • Valuation of unproven technology → stage the ladder (e.g. 20% → 35% → 51%) on milestones, earn-outs.
    • Conflicts between the listed parent and its funds' LPs → independent valuation, independent-director approval of related-party deals.
    • Founder and key-engineer flight after control → keep founder equity and autonomy, retention packages.
    • Export controls on AI chips → screen technology exposure before each step.

B. Platform plus bolt-ons: an industrial AI solutions group

  • Logic: buy control of one mid-sized Chinese industrial-AI or "AI+" solutions company as the platform, then add 4–6 vertical AI application firms (manufacturing, energy, healthcare, logistics), many already in the portfolio. Lenovo supplies hardware and channels; Levima and Joyvio are the first reference customers.
  • Why it fits: serves China's "AI+" push and the group's AI+ application ecosystem; creates a third pillar with recurring software and service revenue.
  • Risks and mitigation:
    • A full price for the platform → choose a platform from within the portfolio where Legend has insight and a relationship.
    • Too many small integrations of people-dependent software teams → light-touch integration (shared sales, cloud and back office; keep product teams), a cap of about two bolt-ons a year.
    • Channel conflict with Lenovo's solutions and services business → define verticals and a reseller agreement up front.

C. Vertical integration: critical AI compute supply chain

  • Logic: take controlling stakes in 2–3 suppliers of components that are scarce or exposed to export controls and that Lenovo's AI servers depend on, such as optical modules and interconnect, liquid cooling and power systems, or domestic accelerator boards. Lenovo's US$54bn AI server backlog provides the anchor demand; Legend owns the assets at holding level and sells to Lenovo at arm's length.
  • Why it fits: supply security is now strategic under export controls; it captures margin along the AI hardware value chain; it uses the group's manufacturing experience (Levima) and portfolio links in AI chips.
  • Risks and mitigation:
    • Capital intensity and hardware cyclicality → minority partners, staged capex, focus on asset-light design-and-assembly assets.
    • Lost flexibility if technology shifts (e.g. copper to optical, new cooling) → keep alternative suppliers; review the program annually.
    • Related-party dependence on Lenovo and conflict with Lenovo's own procurement → arm's-length contracts, independent approval, a target that at least 40% of revenue comes from customers other than Lenovo.
    • Sanctions and export-control exposure → prefer domestic-supply segments; legal screening before each deal.

The technology-access ladder is the entry route for both other programs: the first controlled stakes become the platform for B or the first supply-chain asset for C. Budget: the group set USD 2bn of dry powder (about RMB 14bn) for the M&A team over the program. The split above leaves USD 0.2bn (10%) as a reserve for integration costs and opportunistic deals. Program type set aside

  • Geographic expansion (AI-enabled industrial tech in Europe and Southeast Asia): rejected because of high regulatory risk for a Chinese AI buyer (EU foreign-investment screening, US restrictions) and because the BIL exit signals a retreat from European assets.
  • Consolidation: no fragmented core market to roll up.

3 · Program B in Europe: target landscape and long list

Group decisions: focus on Program B (platform plus bolt-ons) in the European market, across three areas (data-centre efficiency, laptop and edge AI, automation and deployment), plus a reserve for integration and follow-up, all within USD 2bn.

The regulatory reality

  • The new EU Foreign Investment Screening Regulation (EU 2026/1386) entered into force on 16 July 2026 and fully replaces the 2019 regulation from 17 January 2028.
  • Screening becomes mandatory in every member state for a minimum list of sectors: defence and dual-use, semiconductors, quantum, certain AI, critical raw materials and critical (including digital) infrastructure. Closing is prohibited without prior authorisation.
  • Acquisitions through EU subsidiaries of non-EU investors are also covered, so buying via Lenovo's or Legend's European entities does not avoid review.
  • The AI category is narrow (general-purpose AI for space or defence, or with systemic risk under the AI Act), so enterprise automation software is much less exposed than chips.
  • Live precedent: Taiwan's Ennoconn made a mandatory offer for Austria's Kontron (edge AI and IoT computers); an FDI review was opened and Ennoconn did not obtain a majority (July 2026).

Edge-AI chips Very high Minority stakes, partnerships, licensing only
Data-centre efficiency Medium Control of cooling and energy-software vendors; avoid data-centre operators
Automation and deployment Low to medium Control feasible, except public-sector or classified-data specialists

Stage profile: what kind of company is each candidate?

  • Startup (1): Valohai. Seed-funded, small team, product proven but revenue still small. Deal type: full buy-out as a bolt-on or acqui-hire; cheap, but the value sits in a few engineers, so retention packages are essential.
  • Growth, early (3): Iceotope, Asperitas, deepset. Series B or scale-up stage, first proven revenue, VC investors expecting an exit. Deal type: control through buying out VC holders plus fresh capital; this is where Program B finds its platforms.
  • Growth, late (4): Axelera AI, Multiverse Computing, Camunda, Submer. Large rounds and high valuations, often with state or EU investors on the cap table. Deal type: minority stakes, licensing or partnership; control is too expensive and politically sensitive.
  • Mature private (2): EkkoSense, Bizagi. Profitable or near-profitable, private-equity investors in place. Deal type: negotiated buy-out from PE or founders; the cleanest control deals, valued on earnings rather than growth.
  • Listed (1): Kontron. Public company with a free float. Deal type: open-market stake or strategic partnership; a takeover would need a public tender offer and FDI approval, as the Ennoconn case showed.

Implication for the USD 2bn: the control budget goes to the early-growth and mature-private names; late-growth and listed names receive only small, staged minority cheques.

Also removed during verification: Robocorp (FI) was acquired by US-based Sema4.ai in January 2024, so it is no longer an independent target.

Removed during the check: Seldon (UK, MLOps) was on the first draft but was acquired by TrueFoundry in June 2026, so it is no longer available. This is an example of the AI-generated list needing a fact check, as the module asks.

How to make it closeable

  1. A European holding company with independent European management and board.
  2. A European co-investor in the cooling platform to reduce political risk.
  3. Stake ladders that start below control thresholds and step up after clearance; stay non-controlling in chips.
  4. Mitigation offered upfront: EU data residency, no technology transfer to China, European security officers, local jobs.
  5. Narrative: reinvesting the BIL sale proceeds in European technology and jobs.

Risks

  • Screening delays or prohibition: plan 6–12 months per sensitive deal and keep fallback targets.
  • Political precedent (Nexperia 2025, Kontron 2026).
  • European customers' preference for sovereign suppliers.
  • Talent flight after a Chinese takeover: retention packages, founders stay.
  • Rich valuations for European AI infrastructure.

Sizes come from public reports; entries marked "verify", entry costs and the budget split are the group's working estimates for this exercise.

Deal sizes

  • Platforms: USD 300–350m each, control with the founders keeping 20–30% so they stay motivated.
  • Bolt-ons: USD 30–200m, usually 100%, paid partly in earn-outs over two to three years.
  • Minority stakes: USD 50–60m, never above 10% in edge AI, no board control, no access to sensitive technology. Small, passive stakes keep the screening review lighter than for control deals.

Load on the organisation

Each deal is weighted by how much integration it demands: platform = 3 units, large bolt-on = 2, small bolt-on = 1, minority stake = 0.5. The program can carry at most 4 units at a time.

Year Integration load in units Main burden
2027 3.5 Building the European holding company, first FDI filing, Iceotope 100-day plan
2028 4.0 (peak) Second platform plus first bolt-on; the highest-risk year
2029 3.5 Two bolt-ons into existing platforms, plus one minority stake
2030 2.5 Bizagi merged into deepset; Kontron partnership
2031 1.0 Follow-ons only; focus shifts to value creation and exit options

The rule: no new deal is signed while a previous one has a red integration status. That is why the second platform waits until Iceotope's 100-day milestones are met, and why 2028 has only one bolt-on.

Three safeguards built into the structure:

  • FDI firewall. Platforms sit in a European holding company with an EU-majority board and their own security officer, so sensitive data and technology stay in Europe. This is the mitigation offered to regulators up front, not after a review starts.
  • Related-party control. Lenovo is a 32.34% associate, so any commercial agreement between a platform and Lenovo goes through Legend's connected-transaction review under HKEX rules, at arm's length.
  • Separation from Legend's own funds. Legend Capital and Legend Star may not co-invest in or sell to the program without independent valuation and steering-committee approval.

The M&A team

A permanent team of about 14 people, based in Europe (Munich or Zurich), plus external advisers per deal.

External advisers (investment banks, Big Four due diligence, law firms) are bought in per deal; the core team keeps the judgement.

Integration capacity

  • Limit: 4 integration units at a time (platform 3, large bolt-on 2, small bolt-on 1, minority 0.5), as in element 4. One platform integration at a time; at most two bolt-ons per platform per year.
  • Integration approach by stage: startups and early-growth platforms are preserved (own brand, founders stay, Legend adds capital, Lenovo channel and back-office); bolt-ons are absorbed into their platform; mature private targets get financial and process integration but keep their customer-facing teams; minority stakes get no integration, only a commercial agreement.
  • 100-day plan for every control deal: retention of key staff, governance and reporting, two to three quick synergy wins with Lenovo, and the FDI commitments.
  • Gate: the next deal may be signed only when the previous one is green on its 100-day milestones (talent retention, customer churn, reporting in place).

Learning routines

  1. Pre-mortem before signing: the deal team writes down how the deal would fail; the integration lead must sign off.
  2. Red team: a colleague not on the deal argues against it at the steering committee.
  3. Post-deal reviews at 100 days, 12 months and 24 months: actual results compared with the deal model (revenue, synergies, retention, cost).
  4. Playbook: findings go into a written M&A playbook (screening checklist, due diligence templates, 100-day plan, FDI mitigation package), updated after every review.
  5. Annual program review: is the thesis still valid, which areas are working, how should the reserve be used?
  6. Knowledge transfer: integration managers rotate from one deal to the next, so experience stays in the team rather than with advisers.

Criteria for stopping the program

Trigger Threshold Consequence
Regulatory Two control deals blocked, or approved only with conditions that remove control Stop control acquisitions in Europe; switch to minority stakes and partnerships

Synergies Lenovo channel synergies below 50% of plan after 24 months Stop bolt-ons; review whether the vertical link to Lenovo is real

Three levels of response: pause (no new signings, existing integrations continue), stop (no new deals, reserve returned to Legend), exit (sell or list the platforms). The board decides on stop and exit; the steering committee may trigger a pause on its own.
