Case analysis · German chemicals: BASF's approach to Evonik (2026)

BASF / Evonik: a failed bid against six M&A success criteria

BASF's two proposals and withdrawal, scored against six M&A success criteria and compared with the ABN AMRO, Commerzbank and Sunrise / UPC cases. Analysis dated 26 Nov 2026.

Executive summary

On 19 November 2026 BASF ended its pursuit of Evonik after Evonik's executive board rejected two non-binding all-cash proposals: about €22.15 per share (≈€10.3bn) in September and €24 per share (≈€11.2bn, about a 38% premium to the undisturbed price) in October. BASF said it was ready to raise the price moderately, but Evonik's board never engaged and BASF's own view of Evonik's standalone prospects did not justify paying more. Evonik's shares fell 16% the same day.

Scored against six M&A success criteria, the deal had a sound industrial logic and a disciplined price, but failed on synergy evidence, the route to approval and, above all, stakeholder management.

The lesson: in Germany's two-tier, co-determined system, politics, unions and anchor shareholders can matter more than the premium. Walking away was a success for BASF's shareholders; refusing to negotiate may have cost Evonik's about €2.6bn.

The six criteria

1. Strategic rationale: partly met

  • BASF's case: Kamieth called European chemicals consolidation unavoidable and argued Evonik would gain cheaper, more secure access to precursors through BASF's integrated Verbund. The deal would also shift BASF towards higher-margin specialty chemicals (additives, adhesive raw materials, high-performance plastics) and reduce its exposure to volatile bulk chemicals. Antwerp, BASF's second-largest site and Evonik's largest outside Germany, would come under one group.
  • The doubts: sector advisers said the rationale was far from clear and sat poorly with BASF's own growth and cost-cutting strategy; analysts saw limited geographic synergies.
  • The real failure: Evonik formally asked BASF to explain its rationale. The strategy story never landed with the target.

2. Valuation and price discipline: met

  • BASF went from about a 29% premium to about 38%, then stopped. It said Evonik's standalone prospects and risks did not justify a higher valuation, and that under its "Winning Ways" strategy acquisitions are a "could do", not a "must do".
  • Walking away when the price no longer works is a successful M&A outcome for the bidder's shareholders.

3. Synergy case: not met

  • No synergy estimate was published. BASF argued synergies could only be quantified reliably with Evonik's cooperation.
  • Advisers could see production, staff and headquarters synergies, but called the social dimensions "immense".
  • Without a number, BASF could neither justify a higher price to its own shareholders nor show Evonik's shareholders what they were being paid for.

4. Deal structure and route to approval: not met

  • BASF chose a friendly, non-binding route, which required Evonik's board to engage.
  • A hostile bid direct to shareholders would still have needed RAG-Stiftung (about 43–44%). RAG has long said it will keep 25.1% as a blocking minority, the level that blocks a domination agreement after a takeover.
  • So there was no route around both the board and the foundation; non-binding letters were the most BASF could do.

5. Stakeholder and governance management: not met (the decisive factor)

  • State government: NRW premier Wüst opposed the deal and also sits on the body that co-decides the sale of RAG's Evonik shares, so an opponent controlled the key shares.
  • Union: IG BCE backed the opposition; it also holds the deputy chair of Evonik's supervisory board.
  • Target leadership: an interim CEO rarely has a mandate to sell the company.
  • Anchor shareholder: RAG privately favoured deeper talks, but BASF pursued the board instead of first lining up RAG's board and the state government.

6. Integration readiness and social licence: partly met

  • Strong commitments: honour site and collective agreements, no compulsory redundancies in Germany until 2032, significant presence in Essen and NRW, review of the Marl and Wesseling chemical parks.
  • But the pledges remove part of the synergies being bought, and Evonik was itself cutting about 2,150 German jobs.
  • Antitrust overlap in beauty and personal-care ingredients was a further open question.

This explains why RAG-Stiftung publicly regrets the outcome, and why Evonik's board, led by an interim CEO, faces hard questions about refusing even to negotiate.

Lessons

  1. Win the stakeholders before the price. In a two-tier, co-determined system, politics, unions and anchor shareholders can matter more than the premium, as at Commerzbank.
  2. Bring a synergy number. Without one, a bidder can neither raise its price nor put pressure on the target.
  3. Walking away can be a success for the bidder's shareholders.
  4. A board that refuses to negotiate is a risk too: the 16% fall after BASF withdrew suggests Evonik's stance cost its own shareholders.
